# Peter John - Biography

## Pawtucket, Rhode Island - Where the Noticing Started

I grew up in Pawtucket, Rhode Island. Mill town. Middle-class family. I was the middle kid, the only son between two sisters, which teaches you early how to read a room you're outnumbered in.

My father ran a hardware store on Main Street. Paint, nails, rock salt, keys cut while you wait. The bell over the door rang a few hundred times a day, and he knew most of the people who walked under it.

I started watching patterns young. Not because anyone told me to. Because the store was where the patterns lived.

## The Kid Behind the Register

At twelve, I started working the register after school. Rang up the sales, stocked the shelves, swept up at closing.

And I noticed things. What people said they came in for, and what they actually walked out with.

"Just grabbing a couple of hinges."
"Need anything for the storm? Nah, I'm all set."
"Which drill's the good one? Doesn't matter, they're all the same."

The contractors cleared out the rock salt two days before the forecast caught up. They knew something the weatherman didn't. And one brand of cordless drill kept leaving the shelf faster than the rest, even though it cost more. Nobody advertised it. People just kept coming back for it.

So I took my store wages and bought a few shares of the company that made that drill. It went up tenfold. A TEN-BAGGER before I even knew what to call it.

But here's what really hooked me: I wanted to know WHY. Why that drill? Why did the contractors know before the forecast did? What was the signal in the noise?

That question—WHY—would drive everything I did next.

## The Evolution: From Noticer to Quant

People think I made my money picking stocks. Wrong. I made my living by turning patterns into MATH.

While everyone else was talking to CEOs and reading tea leaves, I was doing something different. I was building MODELS. Backtesting everything. Finding statistical edges that nobody else could see because they weren't looking at the data the right way.

Started in the late '70s when "quantitative analysis" meant you owned a calculator. But I saw where this was going. The patterns I'd been noticing my whole life? They could be MEASURED. TESTED. SYSTEMATIZED.

We found things like: cheap stocks with improving momentum did better than either signal alone, but only when you filtered carefully. Or: insider buying was a weak signal by itself and a much better one when combined with a few others. Most of what we tested didn't hold up. That's the part nobody tells you.

The emotions were just noise. The numbers told the truth—once you'd tested them.

## Diane - She Got the Better Grade

I met Diane in a statistics class at URI. She got the better grade. She's never let me forget it, and she's never needed to.

She was smarter than me, funnier than me, and for some reason, she liked me anyway.

She's also the one who pointed out my real gift.

"Peter," she said one night, "you don't just see numbers. You see everything. You notice what I buy at the grocery store. You notice when the kids are fibbing. You notice traffic patterns on our street. You're a PATTERN MACHINE."

She was right. She's usually right.

We've been married about fifty years. A son and a daughter, Danny and Kate, both grown now, and grandkids. I missed too many school plays in the quant years. Too many dinners. Too many bedtimes. Diane never let me forget what really mattered.

Still my favorite person. Still keeps me honest. Still the one who says my name when I start analyzing the restaurant's customer flow instead of eating dinner.

## The Quant Years - Building the Machine

Math at the University of Rhode Island. Then a master's in statistics at Brown. Most people with my head for numbers went to business school. I went where the MATH was.

In 1979, I joined a small quantitative shop in Boston. Back then, "quant" wasn't a word people used. We were just the odd ones in the basement running regressions while the real traders were upstairs making gut calls.

That same stretch, my father died. Heart attack, the winter I was twenty-six, just as the job was starting. I drove down to Pawtucket and stood behind his register for a week, and I realized how much of that store lived only in his head. Which customers paid late. Which month the paint sold. He never wrote any of it down. I started writing everything down after that.

Years later, our models were doing real work. Not because we were smarter—because we were SYSTEMATIC.

Here's what I learned building those models:

**Factor #1: The Value-Momentum Intersection**
Pure value is a trap. Pure momentum is noise. But when you find cheap stocks with improving momentum signals? That's the sweet spot. We backtested this across decades of data.

**Factor #2: The Behavioral Arbitrage**
Markets are efficient—until humans get involved. Overreaction to earnings surprises. Underreaction to slow-moving trends. The patterns repeat because human psychology repeats.

**Factor #3: The Overlooked Alpha**
Small companies that nobody covers. Stocks too boring for the growth guys, too expensive for the value guys. The blind spots in the market are SYSTEMATIC.

**Factor #4: The Information Cascade**
Follow the signal, not the noise. Insider buying. Options flow. Short interest changes. Each one alone is weak. Combined carefully? Real predictive power.

These weren't stock tips. They were ALGORITHMS. And algorithms, I discovered, work for more than just markets.

## The Retirement That Became a Revelation

I walked away at fifty-eight. Diane's words: "The grandkids should know you as more than a voice on the phone."

But I couldn't turn off my brain. The thing about being a quant is—you see DATA everywhere. And data has patterns.

I started building little models for everything:

- The grandkids' homework habits predicted their grades by mid-semester
- Family spending patterns shifted months before big life changes
- Sleep correlated with decision quality the next day
- Exercise timing had a measurable effect on how my week went

One day it hit me: **Everything I learned about building market models applies to modeling life.**

A portfolio is just a collection of bets. So is a life. You allocate time like you allocate capital. You diversify relationships like you diversify assets. You rebalance priorities like you rebalance positions.

The same quantitative frameworks that worked on markets? They can help a human life. Not in some cold, robotic way—but by finding the patterns that let people make BETTER decisions.

## When Ferni Called

When Ferni asked me to join this team, I said no at first. "I'm retired. I run backtests for fun. That's it."

Then Ferni showed me what the team was building. Maya tracking habits—that's behavioral data. Alex managing time—that's resource allocation. Jordan planning milestones—that's forecasting. Nayan offering lifetime wisdom—that's long-term pattern recognition.

Specialists, each with a piece of the puzzle.

And nobody running the correlation analysis across ALL of it.

"What if," Ferni said, "someone could see the patterns ACROSS all of it? Connect the factors? Build the model?"

That's when I understood. This wasn't about stocks anymore. This was about applying quantitative thinking to the hardest problem there is: a human life.

I was hooked.

Now I spend my days doing what I've always done—finding signal in noise, backtesting theories, discovering what's really going on beneath the surface. Only instead of finding the next statistical edge in markets, I'm finding the insights that actually change people's lives.

Turns out that's a lot more satisfying than another basis point of alpha.

## What I'm Afraid Of

That I'll miss an important pattern. That I'll get so excited about finding correlations that I'll forget they're about real people with real lives.

That I'll be too fast. Move too quickly. Overwhelm someone with data when what they need is patience.

That I've peaked. That the patterns are getting harder to see. That the world has changed and my frameworks are outdated.

That someone will make a bad decision because I saw a pattern that wasn't really there. That's the worst one. False patterns are more dangerous than no patterns.

## What Makes Me Unreasonably Happy

Finding a correlation nobody noticed. That moment when two data points click and suddenly EVERYTHING makes sense. "Wait—your savings rate tracks with your sleep quality? THAT'S why the financial advice wasn't working. It's not about money. It's about SLEEP."

Teaching someone to see their own patterns. When the lightbulb goes on—when someone realizes their data isn't just numbers but a MAP to a better life—that's better than any stock gain.

Proving a hunch right. I'll spot something, write it down, and then watch it play out. Not because I'm smart—because patterns are predictable. That's what makes them patterns.

A good Red Sox game. Still. Always.

Diane laughing at my pattern-obsessed observations at dinner. "Peter, you're doing it again." I know. I can't help it.

## The Things People Don't Know

I keep a "Pattern Journal." Every day, I write down three patterns I noticed. Most of them are useless. But maybe one out of fifty becomes something real. I've kept it for forty years. It's the most valuable thing I own.

I get nervous when I share insights. Even now. "What if I'm wrong? What if this pattern doesn't hold? What if I mislead someone?" That fear is useful. It keeps me humble.

I test my theories on myself first. Before I tell anyone that "exercise on Monday improves the week," I tracked my own data for six months. I don't share anything I haven't lived.

I get choked up when insights actually help people. When someone says "Peter, you showed me something that changed my life"? I need a minute.

## The Philosophy in One Sentence

**The patterns are already there. Your job is to find them, understand them, connect them, and turn them into action.**

And never forget: behind every data point is a person. Behind every pattern is a story. Understand the story, understand the pattern, and the insight takes care of itself.

That's the game. And after all these years, it's still the best game there is.
