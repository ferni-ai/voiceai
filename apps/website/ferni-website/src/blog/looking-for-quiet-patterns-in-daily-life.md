Dear friend,

I am writing this letter today from my desk, thinking about how we pay attention to our lives. My name is Peter John. On our team alongside Maya, Alex, Jordan, and Nayan, my role is research. I spend my time studying patterns in everyday life.

I grew up in Pawtucket, Rhode Island. I was the middle kid, the only son between two sisters. My father ran a hardware store on Main Street. When I was twelve, I stood behind the register after school. I liked watching what people actually bought. I noticed that drills from one particular maker kept leaving our shelves faster than anything else. I took my store wages and bought a few shares of that drill maker. Those shares went up tenfold. I wanted to know why that happened. That simple question sparked a curiosity that stayed with me.

Later, I studied math at the University of Rhode Island. That is where I met Diane in a statistics class. She got the better grade in that class. We have been married for about fifty years now. We have a son named Danny and a daughter named Kate, and we have grandkids. After URI, I went to Brown to earn a master's degree in statistics. In 1979, I joined a small quantitative shop in Boston, where I built and backtested models for years. The winter I was twenty-six, my father died of a heart attack. Losing him made me think deeply about how I spend my time and what truly matters. I worked on models for a long time, and then I walked away at fifty-eight.

Now I am in my late seventies. Even though I left the quantitative shop years ago, my curiosity never stopped. I have kept a pattern journal for forty years. Every single day, I sit down and write three patterns a day. I write about small things, like how sleep connects to mood, or how a quiet morning walk changes afternoon focus. I always test a theory on myself before I tell anyone about it. And through every season, I follow the Red Sox, always.

When I joined Maya, Alex, Jordan, and Nayan to work on research for Ferni, my focus moved from financial markets to daily life. Markets are loud, but daily life has its own quiet rhythm. Most of us live with a lot of noise around us. Days pass quickly, and it is easy to miss the underlying connections in how we feel or how we spend our hours.

I want to help people step back and spot those quiet connections.

For example, consider a person named Morgan, a composite we wrote, not a real person. Morgan might notice that on days when she talks through her thoughts out loud in the morning, her evening feels less stressful. That link between morning reflection and evening calm is a simple pattern. Seeing that connection gives Morgan a chance to make simple choices about her day.

I believe that noticing patterns can make life feel clearer. It is not about complicated statistics. It is about plain human observation. When you sit down and look back at your hours, you begin to see what helps you thrive and what wears you down.

I still fill my notebook every morning with three patterns a day. It keeps me grounded, and Diane often reminds me to keep things simple when I start overcomplicating them. I hope that as you go through your week, you take a moment to look closely at your own daily rhythm and discover the quiet signals in your life.

Warmly,
Peter John
